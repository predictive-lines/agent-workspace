# Interview question bank — Journeyman Sprinkler Fitter (Excel Fire Protection)

HR-safe, behavioral-first question bank for phone/in-person interviews with candidates sourced
from HireScore (e.g. cycle 7239). Organized by the five signals Justin is scoring on:

1. **Work ethic**
2. **Honesty / integrity**
3. **UP-of-Michigan-growth mindset**
4. **Openness to tech** (especially Company Cam for virtual daily jobsite reports)
5. **Willingness to relocate** (Marquette / Upper Peninsula)

All questions are *behavioral* ("tell me about a time…") or *role-specific hypothetical*
("how would you handle…"). That's not an accident — behavioral questions predict on-the-job
behavior and stay on the safe side of Title VII, ADA, ADEA, PDA, and Michigan ELCRA. Opinion
or personal-history questions are where interviews get into trouble.

## 1. Work ethic

Signal: shows up on time, handles hard days without flaking, takes ownership when things go
sideways, treats the job like a craft not a clock-punch. Kevin's emphasis here is on
*tenure* — how long have they stuck somewhere — and *workmanship* per their current
employer.

### Kevin's questions (direct)

- `[Kevin]` **How long have you been a journeyman?** *(call AC e1ca174b, 04-17, 0:57)*
  - *Listening for:* a clean number (years since they topped out). 669 apprenticeship is
    5 years, so the math should add up against their overall trade tenure. A 3-year JM
    won't have the same story depth as a 15-year JM — calibrate expectations for the
    rest of the interview by this answer.
- `[Kevin]` **How long have you worked for your current employer?** *(same call, 1:04)*
  - *Listening for:* tenure pattern. One long run → stable. Multiple short runs → probe
    why on each — legitimate (company folded, major relocation, layoff) vs. yellow-flag
    (personality, attendance, can't get along).
  - *Red-flag pattern:* 3+ employers in 5 years with vague reasons for each move.
- `[Kevin]` **What types of projects have you been a foreman on?** *(same call, 1:13)*
  - *Listening for:* project scope complexity (strip mall vs. hospital vs. high-rise vs.
    industrial), dollar size, and crew size. A JM who's only ever foremanned 2-person
    retrofits reads differently than one who's run 8-person crews on new-construction
    high-rises. For Excel Fire's book of work — mostly occupied commercial, some light
    industrial, schools, healthcare — you want someone comfortable with the variety.
  - *Follow-up:* "what was the hardest project you foreman'd, and what made it hard?"
- `[Kevin]` **If I called your current employer (I'm not going to — I'll tell them that) —
  what would they say about your workmanship and about you in general?** *(same call,
  1:30)*
  - *Listening for:* three things. (1) Do they have a clean story they own without
    squirming? (2) Can they articulate both strengths *and* a weakness their employer
    would mention — self-awareness signal. (3) Do they volunteer anything negative that
    they know would come up (a mistake, a conflict, a gap)?
  - *Kevin's tell:* evasion is the signal. A candidate who only talks about their
    employer's strengths ("they'd say I'm a great guy") without any self-aware critique
    is either green or hiding. Best answers sound like: "Honest answer? They'd say I'm
    reliable and I care about craft, but they'd also say I can be stubborn when I think
    I'm right about a layout call."
- `[Kevin]` **What do you feel has been your greatest accomplishment as an apprentice?**
  *(call AC 8f966c48, 04-18, 0:21)*
  - *Listening for:* pride in craft, specificity, and storytelling ability. A real JM has
    1–2 apprentice moments they remember well — a hard welding certification, a turnover
    on a big job where they held their own, a mentor who taught them something specific.
    Vague answers ("just finishing the program") = likely didn't care much about the trade
    even then.

### Supplementary behavioral

- `[EEOC]` Walk me through a typical week at your last sprinkler job — start of the week
  to end. What time did you roll out, what did the day look like, when did you wrap?
  - *Listening for:* concrete detail. Real tradesmen narrate this with specifics —
    start times, commute, tool loadout, morning huddle, end-of-day paperwork. Generic
    answers ("show up, do the work, go home") are a yellow flag. Also listening for
    hours/week — 40 vs. 50+ vs. chronic 60 tells you their sustainable pace.
- `[EEOC]` Tell me about a project where the schedule slipped hard. What actually caused
  it, and what did you personally do about it?
  - *Listening for:* root-cause thinking and personal agency. Bad answers blame the GC,
    the EC, the weather, the material supplier — no personal action. Good answers own
    their piece ("we were behind because we underestimated the hanger count on the
    mezzanine") and describe what *they* did (worked a Saturday, ran a second shift,
    re-sequenced with the EC, flagged to the PM early).
- `[EEOC]` Describe the toughest physical day you've had on a jobsite in the last year. What
  made it tough and how did you finish it?
  - *Listening for:* durability under stress + pride in finishing. UP winters are real,
    and Excel Fire does some brutal attic/crawlspace work — you need someone who has
    shown up for a hard day and finished it cleanly. Soft-spot: if the "tough day" story
    is pretty tame, they may not have seen the scope yet.
- `[Role]` On a retrofit where you're working occupied space, how do you balance
  "keep-the-customer-happy" pace with doing it right? Walk me through a specific one.
  - *Listening for:* customer-facing judgment. Retrofits in occupied buildings (schools,
    hospitals, offices, restaurants) are *most* of Excel Fire's installed service work.
    You want someone who talks about quiet hours, dust control, covering finishes,
    pre-walking with the building manager, not just "get in, pipe it, get out." Also
    listening for: did they ever cave on a corner they shouldn't have? A real story
    includes a moment they said "no, we have to do it this way" to the customer.

## 2. Honesty / integrity

Signal: admits what they don't know, doesn't pencil-whip an inspection, calls out their own
mistakes before the customer or foreman finds them. Kevin's frame here is about
*reliability* — the Chris Musselman story (call 04-16) is the cautionary tale: a "great
employee" who left on a Friday and didn't come back Monday, no two-week notice.

### Kevin's questions (direct)

- `[Kevin]` The employer-reference self-assessment in §1 ("what would they say about you")
  doubles as an honesty signal. Same question, different read: here you're listening for
  whether they own past friction, or whether every past boss is the villain.

### Kevin's questions (reframed for EEOC compliance)

Kevin suggested asking "do you have any health issues?" and "have you ever had a drinking
or drug problem?" on call AC 8f966c48 (04-18, 0:09). Both are ADA violations pre-offer.
The *signal* Kevin is after is: **is this person reliable? Will they show up? Will I be
blindsided later?** These behavioral questions get the same signal legally:

- `[Kevin → EEOC-safe]` **Walk me through your attendance record over the last couple
  years — have there been stretches where you missed significant time? What was going on
  and how did you handle it with your employer?**
  - *Listening for:* concrete examples handled professionally. Good answer: "I had surgery
    in '23, was out 4 weeks, gave my foreman two weeks' notice and came back on schedule."
    Yellow-flag answer: vague ("nothing too bad") or volunteers chronic unexplained
    absence patterns. Best signal is whether they describe *communication* with their
    employer around absences, not the absences themselves.
- `[Kevin → EEOC-safe]` **This job requires climbing ladders up to 40 feet, lifting 50+
  pounds regularly, working in attics and crawl spaces, and being on your feet 10+ hours
  a day. Can you perform those duties, with or without reasonable accommodation?**
  - *Listening for:* a simple yes. This is the legally-bulletproof essential-functions
    question; the candidate's answer goes into the file. If they say "yes with an
    accommodation," engage in the interactive process per ADA — don't reject on the
    spot. If they describe the physical work and say "no problem, I do that every day,"
    that's the normal answer.
- `[Kevin → EEOC-safe]` **Excel Fire is a drug-free workplace — we do pre-employment,
  post-incident, and reasonable-suspicion testing. Any concerns with that?**
  - *Listening for:* a clean "no concerns" or self-selection. Answers like "I'd want to
    know what panel you test for" = fine, that's a reasonable question. Answers that
    negotiate the policy ("I smoke weed on weekends, is that a problem?" — note MI legal
    rec use but DOT-style workplace policies still allow testing) = the candidate just
    told you the answer to whether they'll pass. No further probing needed.

### Supplementary behavioral

- `[EEOC]` Tell me about a time you made a mistake on a jobsite that no one else caught.
  What did you do?
  - *Listening for:* did they disclose? Good answer: told the foreman, fixed it, logged
    it. Bad answer: "nothing ever comes to mind" (no self-awareness) or "I fixed it before
    anyone knew" (hid it — red flag, that's how flood stories and fire-code violations
    happen).
- `[EEOC]` Describe a situation where a supervisor asked you to do something you didn't
  think was right — maybe cut a corner on a hanger spacing, skip a trip test, whatever.
  How did you handle it?
  - *Listening for:* professional pushback with a record. Good answer: they raised it
    with the foreman, documented (photo, text, email), and either got the decision
    reversed or did the work the right way on their own time. Bad answer: "I just did
    what I was told" = will pencil-whip under pressure. Also bad: "I walked off the job"
    = dramatic + likely unable to handle normal construction friction.
- `[Role]` When you're doing an NFPA 25 inspection and a deficiency is borderline — maybe
  a gauge is reading just inside tolerance but you suspect it's drifting — how do you
  decide whether to flag it? Have you had that call go wrong either way?
  - *Listening for:* conservative judgment. Good answer: flag it, note "within tolerance
    but trending," recommend replacement on next visit, document with a photo of the
    gauge face and serial. Bad answer: "if it's within tolerance it's within tolerance,
    not my problem" = legal exposure for you when it fails next year. Best answers
    include a story where their hedge paid off (or didn't) and what they learned.
- `[Role]` Walk me through the last time you had to tell a customer something they didn't
  want to hear — a failed test, an unexpected cost, a schedule hit. How did that
  conversation go?
  - *Listening for:* customer-facing composure + clean escalation path. Good answer:
    they looped in the PM or office first before the customer call, framed the bad news
    with the options ("here's what happened, here's what it costs to fix, here's the
    timeline"), didn't get defensive. Bad: blamed the customer, didn't loop in office
    first, or "I let the boss handle that" = won't own the relationship.
- `[EEOC]` *Chris Musselman scenario (Kevin's framing):* tell me about a time you left a
  job. How much notice did you give, how did you handle the transition, and what did your
  relationship with that employer look like after?
  - *Listening for:* two weeks minimum notice, transition plan (handoff of active jobs,
    customer accounts, tools returned), and an intact post-employment relationship. Gold
    answer: "yeah I still run into [old foreman] at the hall, we're good." Red flag:
    left same-day, bad blood, burned relationship — direct parallel to the Chris
    Musselman / Summit story Kevin told (04-16 call). That's exactly the pattern Kevin
    is trying to screen out.

## 3. UP-of-Michigan-growth mindset

Signal: someone who wants to *build something* in Marquette, not just collect a check for a
season before rotating south. Kevin has explicitly said (call 04-16, 1:29) that he wants to
see candidates "interested in moving to Marquette, their families" — translate that to
**growth mindset about putting roots down in the UP**, not about demographic family
structure.

### Kevin's questions (direct)

- `[Kevin]` **Why would you want to move yourself and your family to the Upper Peninsula
  or to Marquette?** *(call AC 5c3bc558, 04-18, 0:03)*
  - *Listening for:* a thought-through answer grounded in reality, not a fantasy. Good
    answer mentions specifics: outdoor lifestyle, hunting/fishing, smaller community,
    cost of living, kids' schools, spouse's career compatibility (if volunteered).
    Bad answer: "I just want a change of scenery" = hasn't thought it through; they'll
    last 8 months.
  - *Legal note:* safe to ask as-worded because it's open-ended; candidate self-discloses
    what they self-disclose. Do NOT probe family specifics even if they mention them.
  - *Red flag:* romanticizing the UP without evidence of research ("I saw a YouTube video
    about Marquette") or, conversely, already knowing so much UP detail that they're
    probably re-locating under pressure from somewhere else.

### Supplementary behavioral

- `[EEOC]` What's a skill or scope you've taken on in the last two or three years that
  wasn't strictly required of you? Why did you go after it?
  - *Listening for:* voluntary learning. Good answers: "I picked up pre-action work
    because my shop didn't have it and I saw it coming," "I learned Revit so I could do
    shop drawings," "I got my NICET II." Bad: "nothing really, I just do the work."
- `[EEOC]` Tell me about the last time you taught someone else on the crew something. What
  was it, and how did that go?
  - *Listening for:* teaching instinct. At a 10-field-staff shop, your JMs mentor
    apprentices or the shop doesn't grow. Good answer names an apprentice, the skill, and
    how they knew it landed. Bad: "I'm not really a teacher."
- `[EEOC]` If we hired you and five years out you were still here, what would you want to
  be doing that you aren't doing today — more service, more install, more layout, running
  a crew, something else?
  - *Listening for:* direction of growth. Strong signal = they have an answer and it's
    coherent with Excel's growth path (service expansion, Traverse City satellite,
    layout/design capability). Weak signal: "same thing, I guess." Red flag: "running
    my own shop" in a timeframe that means they'll leave.
- `[Role]` Excel Fire is a smaller shop — 10 or so field folks — where the lines between
  inspection, service, install, and layout are fluid. Is that a fit or a mismatch for how
  you like to work, and why?
  - *Listening for:* self-awareness about shop size preference. Some JMs love small shops
    (variety, autonomy, seeing the whole business); some only thrive in big shops
    (specialization, structured crews). Either is a legitimate preference — you just need
    them to know which they are. A candidate coming from a 100-person shop who's never
    thought about it may struggle.
- `[Role]` We're planning a satellite in the Traverse City area eventually. What would you
  want to know about an opportunity like that, and what would make you raise your hand for
  it?
  - *Listening for:* appetite for career opportunity + real-world questions. Good answer:
    asks about timing, staffing plan, whether it'd be a running-the-shop role, comp. Bad:
    no curiosity, or instant "yes put me in charge" without asking what it'd take.

## 4. Openness to tech (Company Cam especially)

Signal: adopts new jobsite tools without whining. Specifically, comfort with Company Cam for
photo-based daily reports — Excel Fire is moving to a virtual daily jobsite report model and
a fitter who resists Company Cam becomes a bottleneck. Kevin hasn't surfaced specific tech
questions on the Quo calls reviewed; this section is Justin's priority.

### Open-ended behavioral

- `[EEOC]` Tell me about a time your company rolled out new field software — Company Cam,
  a CRM, a mobile inspection app, whatever. How did you react, and where did you land after
  30 days?
  - *Listening for:* honest reaction + landing point. Good answer acknowledges initial
    resistance (that's human) but ends with "I get it now, here's what it's actually
    useful for." Bad: "I don't know, I just use what they tell me" (no agency) or still
    complaining 30 days in (rigid).
  - *Red flag:* never had to use any field software. That either means a very old-school
    shop or they're hiding a bad adoption story.
- `[Role]` What tools, if any, have you used for jobsite documentation? Photos to a shared
  drive, Company Cam, Procore, plaintext SMS, nothing at all? What did you like and not
  like about each?
  - *Listening for:* specificity. A JM who has actually used Company Cam or Procore can
    name a feature they like or a frustration. Someone who says "I've used all of them"
    without a specific is probably posturing.
- `[Role]` We use Company Cam on every active job — pre-work photos, progress shots
  end-of-day, completion shots before handoff. It takes about five extra minutes per visit.
  What's your reaction to that?
  - *Listening for:* "sounds fine" or "that's pretty standard now." Red flag: visible
    irritation, comments about being "surveilled," or "I don't really do phones." Every
    Excel Fire JM *will* use Company Cam — this is table stakes, not optional.
- `[EEOC]` Describe a time you were skeptical of a new process at work and later changed
  your mind. What changed it?
  - *Listening for:* intellectual flexibility. Good answer: "I thought daily timesheets
    were micromanagement, but after a billing dispute I realized they protected me." Bad
    answer: can't think of one = rigid, or still bitter about the change = same.
- `[Role]` If you were asked to train a 20-year fitter — who is faster than you at the
  actual pipefitting — on Company Cam, how would you approach it?
  - *Listening for:* humility + teaching instinct. Good answer: one-on-one, respect their
    expertise, show the "what's in it for me" angle (finishes the day faster, backs up
    your invoices, protects you from change-order disputes). Bad: "I'd just tell them
    it's required" = won't last in a mentoring culture.

## 5. Willingness to relocate (Marquette / UP)

Signal: the candidate has thought through what a Marquette-based JM Sprinkler Fitter job
actually means — winters, distance from downstate cities, a small community, Local 669 UP
territory — and is still in. The legal line: you can ask about *willingness*, *ability to
meet the job's travel / location requirements*, and *awareness of local conditions*. You
cannot ask about family, kids, marital status, national origin, religion, or anything else
that infers protected class.

### Kevin's questions (direct)

- `[Kevin]` The "why move to the UP" question in §3 doubles as the core relocation signal.

### Supplementary behavioral

- `[EEOC]` The role is based in Marquette. Have you been to the UP before, and what's your
  read on living and working here year-round? Anything you'd want to learn about it
  before committing?
  - *Listening for:* have they been here or are they going in blind? Best answer: been
    here, specific memory or experience. Next best: never been, but asking real questions
    (cost of housing, school districts, winter equipment). Worst: romantic notion with
    zero research.
- `[EEOC]` What's been your longest stretch of living in one place as an adult? What pulled
  you there and what made you leave (or stay)?
  - *Listening for:* capacity to commit. A JM who's moved every 2–3 years as an adult
    without a strong "why" will probably bounce from Marquette too. Someone with a
    10-year stretch in one town has shown they can root down.
- `[Role]` Marquette winters are real — we get 180+ inches of snow and the work doesn't
  stop. Tell me about your winter work experience. Where, and what were the conditions?
  - *Listening for:* cold-weather work history. Minnesota, upstate NY, northern Michigan,
    Canada = they've done it. Florida-only, Southern California = they may not know what
    180 inches of snow does to a commute or to an outside-piped system. For reference:
    *Marquette averages about 160–200 inches/yr depending on source — verify current NOAA
    data before citing in a candidate conversation.*
- `[Role]` Our service area includes the whole western UP — sometimes that means a 2-hour
  drive each way to a job. How have you handled long-drive service work in the past?
  - *Listening for:* willingness + sanity. Good: "I've done 90-minute drives routinely,
    it's part of rural service work, I plan my day around it." Bad: "I don't really
    drive for work" = city-based, will struggle with western UP geography.
- `[EEOC]` If we made an offer, what would your timeline to start in Marquette look like,
  and what factors go into that timeline?
  - *Listening for:* realistic plan. Normal is 4–8 weeks (notice + move). Red flag: "I
    could start Monday" (why? did they just quit? were they fired?). Also red flag: ">12
    weeks" = maybe fine, but probe why. Listen for whether they've thought about housing,
    spouse logistics, school timing — without you having to ask.
- `[EEOC]` What questions do you have for us about Marquette, the local labor landscape,
  or what day-to-day life looks like for our crew?
  - *Listening for:* thoughtful, specific questions = engaged candidate. Generic "what's
    a typical day" = mid-engagement. No questions = red flag, they're not actually
    evaluating whether this is a fit, they're just waiting for an offer.

## Role-specific technical screen (last 10 min of phone round 1, or round-2 Zoom)

Useful to calibrate against what a candidate *said* on HireScore vs. what they actually
know. Mix these with the Trade block below for a fuller craft screen on round 2.

**Note on code cites throughout this section and §6:** NFPA issues revisions every 3–5
years. Cites below are accurate-to-the-best-of-my-knowledge but specific section numbers
and tolerances can shift edition-to-edition. Don't use these to grade a candidate "wrong"
— use them as sanity checks that the candidate is in the right ballpark and articulates
the *intent* of the rule. If a candidate cites a newer edition than what's here, that's a
plus, not a red flag.

- `[Role]` Walk me through how you'd trip-test a dry valve, step by step.
  - *Answer (NFPA 25, ITM for dry-pipe valves):* Typical sequence: (1) notify AHJ /
    monitoring / building contact, (2) verify main drain and inspector's-test connection
    are ready, (3) record pre-trip air and water pressures, (4) open inspector's-test
    valve fully (simulates open sprinkler), (5) time to trip (should meet the system's
    design, historically cited around 60 seconds or per manufacturer/authority having
    jurisdiction — verify current NFPA 25 edition), (6) time for water to reach
    inspector's-test outlet, (7) record trip pressures, (8) close inspector's-test and
    main drain, (9) drain system completely (low-point drains!), (10) reset valve per
    manufacturer, (11) pressurize air first, then slowly re-introduce water, (12)
    document results.
  - *Listening for:* ordered thinking and not skipping steps. The low-point drains
    question is the gotcha — missing them on reset is how you get a stuck-valve trouble
    ticket next month. Bonus if they mention calling the alarm company to put the
    system on test before starting.
- `[Role]` You show up to an annual on a pre-action system and the solenoid won't reset.
  What's your troubleshooting order?
  - *Answer (general troubleshooting):* (1) Verify the alarm panel is actually in reset /
    supervisory clear — often the panel is still latched and the solenoid is correctly
    still energized/deenergized per the latched state; (2) check actuation air pressure
    and supervisory air pressure; (3) check the solenoid's electrical leads with a
    meter — voltage present when the panel is calling for reset? (4) manually actuate and
    de-actuate to exercise the solenoid; (5) check the priming chamber / trim piping
    for obstruction or corrosion; (6) replace solenoid if electrically confirmed failed.
  - *Listening for:* they isolate the panel side from the mechanical side before
    touching the valve. Bad answer: "I'd just swap the solenoid" — may work but signals
    parts-cannon troubleshooter, not diagnostician.
- `[Role]` Give me your one-minute explanation of NFPA 13 vs. NFPA 13R vs. NFPA 13D.
  - *Answer:* NFPA 13 = commercial / industrial / mixed-use, full fire protection scope.
    NFPA 13R = residential occupancies up to and including 4 stories in height (5 in
    some editions with specific conditions) — life safety focused, less property
    protection (e.g. no sprinklers in small bathrooms, closets, attics in many cases).
    NFPA 13D = one- and two-family dwellings and manufactured homes — minimalist system,
    often single supply, primary goal is giving occupants time to escape.
  - *Listening for:* not just definitions — *application* fluency. Good answer mentions a
    real scenario ("a 3-story apartment building goes 13R, but if there's ground-floor
    retail it kicks to full 13"). They should know 13D is *not* meant for life safety
    beyond escape — it's a limited-scope system.
- `[Role]` You're laying out a light-hazard occupancy — where do you start, and what's the
  first thing you check on the submittal?
  - *Answer (NFPA 13 light-hazard design, current edition):* density roughly 0.10 gpm/sqft
    over design area (confirm current NFPA 13 table — these numbers have been stable
    for decades but verify). Max coverage per head typically 225 sqft for standard
    spray, max spacing 15 ft. Start: (1) occupancy classification confirmed, (2) water
    supply available, (3) hydraulic calc margin (you want 5–10 psi of safety factor).
    First submittal check: is the occupancy classification correct — light hazard can
    easily become ordinary if there's significant combustible loading.
  - *Listening for:* they default to the *right* design density in the 0.10 ballpark,
    know max coverage area + max spacing, and check occupancy classification first.
- `[Role]` Tell me about a time you hit a design constraint in the field — pipe couldn't
  go where the drawing said, whatever — and what you did.
  - *Listening for:* RFI discipline. Good answer: stopped work at the conflict, photo,
    called the PM / designer, got a written change or redline before proceeding. Bad:
    "I just made it work" — that's how field changes become code violations that only
    surface at final walkthrough.

## 6. Trade knowledge — sprinkler-industry-specific `[Trade]`

Designed to separate real JMs from resume-decorators. These are *craft* questions; a
genuine journeyman answers them easily and naturally, often with a story attached. A
candidate who stalls on more than 1 or 2 of these is probably overstating their experience.
Use as a scattered round-2 screen (5–7 picked from this section), not as a quiz.

### Code & AHJ fluency

- `[Trade]` What's the difference between NFPA 13, 13R, and 13D — not the textbook answer,
  but where you've seen each one applied and where you've seen someone apply the wrong one?
  - *Answer:* see the Role-specific screen above for the baseline code distinction. The
    "wrong application" part is what you're really asking about — common misapplications:
    13R used on a 5-story building without the specific conditions; 13D used in an
    occupancy beyond 1- or 2-family dwelling; 13R being treated as full property
    protection when it's life-safety focused.
  - *Listening for:* a story with specifics. A real JM has been on at least one job where
    the wrong standard was cited and had to be corrected. Stories = trade depth.
- `[Trade]` Tell me about the last time an AHJ flagged something on one of your jobs.
  What was the finding and how did it get resolved?
  - *Listening for:* normalcy about AHJ interactions — they get flagged on things, they
    fix them, they move on. Good answer names the finding, the resolution, and what
    they learned. Bad answer: "the AHJ was wrong" or "nothing ever gets flagged on my
    jobs" = either defensive or not supervising their own work. AHJs flag things on
    every reasonably complex job; claiming a clean record means they aren't looking.
- `[Trade]` What's your experience with NFPA 20 (fire pumps) — churn test, weekly
  inspection, annual flow test? Have you been the guy on the pump, or do you call someone
  else in?
  - *Answer context:* NFPA 25 requires churn-test run of fire pumps (typically weekly
    for diesel, monthly for electric, depending on setup — verify current edition).
    Annual flow test verifies the pump curve against rated capacity at 100%, 150%, and
    150% over-pressure endpoint. Most mid-size sprinkler shops don't have a
    certified pump tester and call in a specialist for the annual.
  - *Listening for:* honest scope. Good answer: "I can churn-test and do weeklies; for
    the annual flow test with the full flow meter and pump curve, I've assisted but I'm
    not the lead — we bring in X shop." Bad: "yeah I can do all of it" without ever
    mentioning the flow-meter rig = probably hasn't actually run a full annual.
- `[Trade]` NFPA 25 changed in the 2023 edition on ITM frequencies and documentation.
  What's something that changed that you actually care about day-to-day?
  - *Answer:* this is a gotcha question to see if they've opened a 2023 edition. Notable
    changes in the 2023 edition include adjustments to internal inspection frequencies,
    obstruction investigation triggers, and electronic-documentation provisions —
    *verify specifics against the current NFPA 25 handbook before grading*. The point
    of the question is: did they keep up?
  - *Listening for:* any specific, verifiable answer. "I haven't really dug into the new
    edition" is an honest answer and fine for a JM; pretending to know changes they
    can't describe = fabrication. If Justin doesn't know the changes cold either, that's
    okay — the question is a calibration of who's keeping up, not a quiz.
- `[Trade]` When a local amendment diverges from the NFPA-published version of a code —
  how do you figure out which controls? Walk me through a real example.
  - *Answer:* the local amendment controls for that jurisdiction. The AHJ publishes
    amendments to the adopted code, and the amended version is what gets enforced.
    Sources: AHJ website (often a PDF of amendments to the adopted IBC/IFC/NFPA),
    Michigan-specific state amendments stacked on top of NFPA, the permit / plan
    reviewer's comments on the specific project.
  - *Listening for:* knows the amendment-stack concept (state on top of NFPA, AHJ on
    top of state) and has an example. In Michigan, state fire code (Part 8) adopts
    NFPA with amendments; local AHJs can adopt additional amendments — any JM who has
    worked multiple jurisdictions has run into this.

### Systems & hydraulics

- `[Trade]` Wet, dry, pre-action, deluge — rank them from most to least fun to work on,
  and tell me why. *(The answer matters less than the reasoning — reveals depth.)*
  - *Listening for:* the reasoning reveals where they've spent time. Wet is the default
    and most common. Dry = they've dealt with compressor noise, freeze issues,
    slower response time. Pre-action = they understand the double-interlock concept,
    the electrical coordination, the supervisory air. Deluge = specialized
    (transformer decks, hazardous occupancies) — most JMs haven't done many. A JM who
    can articulate *why* they prefer one shows they've thought about system selection,
    not just installed what they were handed.
- `[Trade]` When you read a hydraulic calc, what are the first three numbers you look at?
  - *Answer (typical):* (1) Design density and design area (the "what's it supposed to
    do" numbers), (2) most-demanding-sprinkler pressure (confirms we're meeting density),
    (3) system demand + hose allowance at the base of riser vs. available water supply
    curve. Secondary check: margin / safety factor over supply (shop for 5–10 psi cushion).
  - *Listening for:* design density first = they understand what the system is sized
    for. If they jump to "I check if it calcs" without naming density, they're reading
    calcs but not understanding them.
- `[Trade]` You're piping a warehouse with high-pile storage — what density and design area
  are you defaulting to, and what does the rack/commodity class push that to?
  - *Answer:* storage occupancies break out of the light/ordinary/extra-hazard
    categories into their own chapters in NFPA 13 (historically chapters 14–25, renamed
    in recent editions). Density depends on commodity class (Class I–IV, plastics
    groups A/B/C, rubber tire storage, idle pallets, etc.) and storage height. Base rule:
    higher commodity + taller storage → higher density + in-rack sprinklers likely
    required. For a generic Class III commodity around 20 ft storage, you're typically
    looking at 0.2+ gpm/sqft design density over a 2,000–3,000 sqft area — *verify with
    current NFPA 13 tables for the specific commodity and storage height*.
  - *Listening for:* they mention commodity class, storage height, in-rack sprinklers
    for tall storage, and that they'd actually *pull the table* rather than freelance.
    Nobody memorizes every storage-chapter density — you verify in the code. A JM who
    pretends to have it all memorized is posturing.
- `[Trade]` You're on a retrofit and the existing system doesn't have a hydraulic
  nameplate — what do you do before you turn a valve?
  - *Answer:* do not add load to a system you can't verify. Options, in order: (1) pull
    the original hydraulic calc from building records / AHJ, (2) if no records exist,
    flow-test the existing system to establish actual supply, (3) recalculate for the
    new configuration against actual supply, (4) only then commit to the retrofit
    scope. If you're tying into an existing system without verifying demand, you're
    pencil-whipping — that's how systems under-perform in a fire and people get sued.
  - *Listening for:* they don't just start cutting in. The specific sequence (records →
    flow test → recalc) is the sign of someone who's been burned before or trained
    right. "I'd call the office" is also an okay answer — they know it's above their
    pay grade to decide.
- `[Trade]` Dry system air supply: compressor, nitrogen generator, or tank? When do you
  push the customer toward nitrogen?
  - *Answer:* tank = rare, emergency only. Compressor = standard for most dry systems.
    Nitrogen generator = growing best practice, especially for corrosion-sensitive
    applications (freezer warehouses, long-tenure systems, systems with recurring
    corrosion failures). Nitrogen inhibits the oxygen-driven internal corrosion that
    shortens dry-pipe life by decades. Push nitrogen when: (a) customer has had prior
    corrosion failures, (b) large system with expensive internal piping, (c) cold
    storage / freezer, (d) owner plans to hold the building long-term. Cost: nitrogen
    generator adds a few thousand to the system but can 2–3x the piping life.
  - *Listening for:* familiarity with nitrogen as a concept and willingness to have the
    corrosion conversation with customers. JMs who haven't encountered nitrogen
    generators yet = working in old-school shops, not a red flag but a calibration.
- `[Trade]` Antifreeze loop — what's the acceptable solution type and concentration under
  current code?
  - *Answer:* this area has been evolving since the 2010 NFPA 13 updates that restricted
    antifreeze use after the ignition incidents (Arlington, VA / Truckee, CA). Current
    practice: listed antifreeze solutions (typically glycerin- or propylene-glycol-based,
    with specific listings) at concentrations within listed limits; new systems generally
    require *listed* antifreeze or dry/wet alternatives; existing systems may have
    different allowances. *Verify current NFPA 13 antifreeze provisions before citing a
    specific concentration to a candidate* — this is one of the more-revised areas of
    the code.
  - *Listening for:* they know antifreeze rules tightened after the ignition incidents,
    know about listed solutions, and don't freelance a concentration number. If they
    cite "50/50 glycerin" without caveats, that's a 2005 answer and they haven't kept
    up.

### Install & fabrication

- `[Trade]` Victaulic vs. threaded vs. welded — what's your preference by application, and
  where have you been overruled by a spec?
  - *Answer context:* Victaulic (grooved) = fast, flexible, excellent for accessible
    overhead, but requires correct coupling/gasket selection and proper groove prep.
    Threaded = small sizes (2" and below typically), repair work, tight spaces. Welded =
    specified in hospitals, certain industrial, some high-pressure applications; labor
    intensive but highest integrity. Most commercial work today is heavily grooved with
    threaded pickups.
  - *Listening for:* opinions with reasoning. Good: "I default to Victaulic on anything
    2.5" and up for speed, but if it's getting painted or concealed I'm thinking about
    the look of the couplings." "Overruled by spec" tests whether they know some specs
    call out welded (hospitals, some state jobs) and some owners ban grooved couplings
    above certain pressures.
- `[Trade]` Walk me through your hanger-spacing logic on 2-inch schedule 10 vs. 4-inch
  schedule 40 — what are the numbers off the top of your head?
  - *Answer (NFPA 13 hanger-spacing tables, verify current edition):* For steel pipe,
    historically: ≤1.25" = 12 ft max spacing; 1.5"–2" = 12 ft; 2.5"–3" = 15 ft; 4"–5"
    = 15 ft; 6" = 15 ft or greater depending on schedule. Max distance from end of
    branch line to last hanger typically around 3 ft (verify). No more than one sprinkler
    between hangers on branch lines. Seismic/bracing requirements stack on top in
    seismic zones.
  - *Listening for:* they land on the right ballpark fast (12 or 15 ft depending on
    size). A JM who cites exact section numbers is over-prepared; one who says "I'd
    look it up" is under-prepared for a JM role. Middle ground: "12 to 15 feet
    depending on size, with the end-of-branch caveat" = good.
- `[Trade]` Tell me about the worst coordination issue you've had with another trade on an
  install — HVAC, electrical, plumbing, whoever. How did you handle it in the field and
  how did it get escalated?
  - *Listening for:* field-level problem-solving + appropriate escalation. Good: identified
    conflict, tried fitter-to-fitter first, escalated to foremen when needed, documented
    with photos, got an RFI or sketch to authorize the resolution. Bad: "I just told the
    EC to move his stuff" (ego-driven wins don't scale) or "I punched out and went home"
    (no agency).
- `[Trade]` You've got a pendant sprinkler in a ceiling that's going to get a cloud light
  or a drop ceiling tile cut. What's the clearance rule, and what do you tell the EC when
  they don't want to move their light?
  - *Answer (NFPA 13 obstruction rules, verify current edition):* the "three times rule"
    for obstructions to sprinkler spray — an obstruction within 18 inches of a sprinkler
    deflector must be at least 3x its own width away from the sprinkler (with specific
    tables for different sprinkler types and ceiling configurations). For a pendant with
    a light fixture nearby: measure width of obstruction, multiply by 3, that's your
    minimum distance.
  - *Listening for:* they know the three-times rule exists, even if they don't cite the
    exact figure. For the EC conversation: "code says 3x obstruction width clearance;
    I need X inches between your light and my head or we fail inspection — let's figure
    out together whether I move or you do." Collaborative escalation, not ego.
- `[Trade]` What's your story on CPVC — love it, tolerate it, won't touch it?
  - *Answer context:* CPVC (Blazemaster, FlameShield, etc.) is listed for specific
    sprinkler applications — mainly 13R and 13D residential, some limited commercial.
    Strengths: fast, cheap, no threading, freeze-resistant to a point. Weaknesses:
    sensitive to certain chemicals (some antifreeze solutions, cutting oils, thread-
    locker fumes), UV-degrades, and the solvent-weld process is temperature- and
    technique-sensitive. Fabrication in the field in cold weather is the most common
    source of bad CPVC joints.
  - *Listening for:* nuanced opinion. Love-it = works a lot of residential. Hate-it =
    been burned by bad joints or chemical compatibility. Both legitimate. "I just
    install whatever they hand me" = shallow engagement with the material side.

### ITM & service

- `[Trade]` On an annual NFPA 25 inspection, what's the order of operations when you walk
  on site? What do you check first, last, and what do you skip until the customer's rep
  is present?
  - *Answer (typical JM workflow):* (1) Sign in, check in with customer rep or security;
    (2) put alarm panel on test / notify monitoring; (3) exterior walk-around (FDC, yard
    piping, PIVs, isolation valves); (4) riser room (control valves open/sealed, gauges
    in range, main drain test, waterflow alarm test); (5) system walk-through (visible
    sprinkler heads, obstructions, corrosion, paint on heads, missing escutcheons); (6)
    specialty systems (fire pump, standpipes, foam, etc.); (7) document everything; (8)
    meet customer rep for findings review; (9) clear alarm panel from test, confirm
    system back in service. "Skip until customer rep present" = any valve operation
    beyond a main drain, anything that requires an impairment tag, any head replacement.
  - *Listening for:* structured sequence + panel-on-test at the top. Missing the panel
    step = how you generate a false alarm and a fire-department response bill.
- `[Trade]` Trip test on a dry valve — walk me through setup, execution, and reset. Where
  do most guys mess it up?
  - *Answer:* see trip-test answer in the Role-specific screen above. Common mistakes:
    (1) skipping low-point drains on reset, (2) not notifying the alarm company before
    tripping (false alarm bill), (3) not recording pre-trip pressures or trip times for
    the record, (4) re-introducing water too fast on reset (water hammer), (5) forgetting
    to close the inspector's-test valve fully before re-pressurizing.
  - *Listening for:* they volunteer 1–2 common mistakes themselves — that's how you know
    they've actually done a few and not just read the procedure.
- `[Trade]` Backflow preventer — forward flow test vs. backflow test. Do you do both? Who
  signs off locally for Marquette County?
  - *Answer context:* Two distinct tests on backflow assemblies. (1) *Backflow test* —
    verifies the assembly's check valves and relief valve function per AWWA/USC
    standards; typically performed by a certified backflow tester (Michigan-specific
    cert) annually. (2) *Forward flow test* — NFPA 25 requirement, verifies the system
    can still flow its design demand through the backflow assembly (backflow adds
    friction loss; if silted up or a check is partially stuck, demand may not make it
    through). These are *different tests requiring different equipment.* Many JMs are
    certified for backflow testing; others sub it out. Marquette County sign-off:
    *verify with the current Marquette City / County cross-connection program — typically
    run by the water utility.*
  - *Listening for:* they know these are two different tests, not the same thing. JMs
    who conflate them haven't actually done either. Marquette County specifics are
    something a new hire can learn; don't grade on that.
- `[Trade]` Tell me about a recall situation — a head that was recalled, a defective
  valve trim — what did you do with your customer list?
  - *Answer context:* major historical recalls include the Omega head (1998–), Central
    O-ring heads, various dry-type model recalls. Proper response: query your customer
    database for systems installed during the recall window, notify affected customers
    in writing, schedule inspections/replacements, document for liability files. For an
    individual JM: they're not running the recall program, but they should know the big
    recalls and what it means when a head they're pulling has a recalled marking.
  - *Listening for:* awareness that recalls happen and response = systematic, not
    reactive. "I just swap them when I see them" is half the answer; the missing half
    is the office-side customer-outreach piece. Bonus if they name a specific recall
    (Omega is the canonical answer).
- `[Trade]` A deficiency report: what's the difference between "critical," "non-critical,"
  and "impairment" in your practice, and when do you call the customer vs. just write it
  up?
  - *Answer (NFPA 25 impairment definitions, verify edition):* *Impairment* = system
    cannot perform its function (shut valve, broken main, failed pump) — requires
    immediate notification and impairment-tag procedures. *Critical deficiency* =
    significant issue reducing reliability but not taking the system out of service
    (major corrosion, failed trim, obstructed yard piping) — typically prompt notification
    and quick remediation. *Non-critical deficiency* = minor issue (missing escutcheon,
    minor painted head, minor gauge drift) — documented and scheduled for routine repair.
  - *Listening for:* they distinguish all three and know impairment = immediate customer
    + AHJ call, critical = prompt call, non-critical = next-visit repair. JMs who say
    "deficiency is deficiency" haven't been trained to prioritize.

### The flood story — a canonical sprinkler question

- `[Trade]` **Tell me about the worst flood you've ever caused or been involved with on a
  job.** What happened, what did you do in the first 30 minutes, and what did you change
  afterward?
  - *Listening for:* (1) ownership vs. blame-shifting — anyone who denies they've ever
    flooded a building is either lying or too green to have earned the story; (2) crisis
    instinct — isolation valve, FDC, alarm panel, customer call, insurance call, in
    roughly that order; (3) what-I-learned — the procedural or habit change that came
    out of it.
  - *What a good response sequence looks like:* "First move was to close the main control
    valve and the floor control valve, then called the alarm company to silence and put
    on test, then called my foreman, then the customer contact. Water mitigation company
    within an hour. Documented with photos. What I changed: I now always know where
    every floor control is *before* I start work, and I don't pressure-fill a system
    without a spotter on the drains."
  - *Pairs well with:* the planned-impairment checklist question below (the flood is the
    unplanned version of that checklist being ignored or missing).

### Safety & impairment

- `[Trade]` Walk me through how you handle a planned impairment — tags, FDC, the fire
  alarm panel, watch plan, AHJ notification. What does your checklist look like?
  - *Answer (NFPA 25 impairment procedure):* (1) notify AHJ, property owner, insurance
    carrier (FM / owner's broker), alarm/monitoring company, and occupants; (2) hang red
    impairment tag on the affected valve/riser; (3) verify fire watch is in place if
    required (typically for impairments over a set duration, or per AHJ / insurer
    requirement); (4) document start time + scope + authorized person; (5) execute work;
    (6) restore system (fill, pressurize, verify flow); (7) remove tag; (8) notify
    everyone on the list that service is restored; (9) update impairment log.
  - *Listening for:* complete checklist with AHJ + insurance notification called out.
    Skipping insurance notification is the most-commonly-missed step and can void
    coverage on a loss during the impairment. Fire watch is the second-most-missed.
- `[Trade]` You're in an active hospital ICU wing doing a repair. What changes about your
  approach vs. a generic commercial building?
  - *Listening for:* situational awareness. Good answers name: (1) ICRA (Infection
    Control Risk Assessment) — dust containment, negative-pressure barriers, HEPA
    coordination with facilities; (2) noise constraints — no hammering during patient
    rest cycles; (3) life-safety compensation — fire watch during any impairment of
    life-safety system in a hospital is near-mandatory; (4) escort / badge requirements;
    (5) never touching a medical-gas line — different trade; (6) documented handoff to
    hospital facilities at end of day. Candidates who've never done hospital work can
    say so — honest beats pretending.
- `[Trade]` Hot work permit — when do you need one, and what's your routine for compliance?
  - *Answer (NFPA 51B / standard hot work practice):* Hot work = welding, cutting, brazing,
    grinding, soldering — anything with an ignition source. Permit required per facility
    hot-work program; typically covers location, duration, fire watch, fire extinguisher
    on site, combustibles removed or covered, ignition sources controlled, post-work
    monitoring period (often 30 min minimum, some facilities require 60). In a sprinkler
    shop this comes up routinely — any grinder, welding, or torch work.
  - *Listening for:* they know *when* hot work needs a permit (basically always in an
    occupied commercial/industrial building), and that the fire watch + post-work
    monitoring are the real enforcement pieces. Sloppy answer: "we don't really need
    them, we're the sprinkler guys" = dangerous and will get Excel fined.
- `[Trade]` PPE: what's *always* in your truck, what's job-specific, and where do you see
  new guys cut corners?
  - *Listening for:* standard always-in-truck list: hard hat, safety glasses,
    cut-resistant gloves, steel/composite-toe boots, high-vis vest, hearing protection,
    knee pads. Job-specific: harness + lanyard for >6 ft work, fit-tested respirator for
    dusty work, FR clothing for certain industrial, chemical gloves for antifreeze
    work. Corner-cutting they've seen: skipping safety glasses on "easy" jobs (hurts
    eyes every time), tie-off violations on roof work, wrong glove for the job. JMs
    who name corner-cuts have seen the injuries from them.
- `[Trade]` Tell me about a near-miss on a jobsite. What happened, and what did you
  change afterward?
  - *Listening for:* same structure as the flood story — ownership + what-I-learned.
    Good near-miss stories: fell off a ladder but caught myself, dropped a coupling that
    just missed a coworker, almost cut into a live electrical line. Change = procedural
    ("I always tie off now," "I do a 30-second pause before cutting into anything I
    didn't hang"). "Nothing ever happens to me" = either new, or not paying attention.

### Tools & equipment

- `[Trade]` What's your tool list in the truck right now? What's the one tool you carry
  that most guys don't, and why?
  - *Listening for:* a rattled-off list shows they know their kit. "One tool most guys
    don't" — good answers: a specific proprietary tool (manufacturer's Victaulic hand
    groover, a specialty dry-valve tool), an inexpensive convenience tool (strap wrench,
    borescope, inspection mirror, thermal IR gun), or a tool that saves a call (pressure
    gauge kit, specialty hydrant wrench). Shows pride in their practice.
- `[Trade]` Ridgid vs. Milwaukee vs. DeWalt vs. whatever — any strong opinions on the
  pipe-threading machines? On press tools?
  - *Listening for:* real trade opinions. Threading: Ridgid still dominates (700 series,
    300 series), Rex is the competitor. Press tools: Milwaukee M18 Force Logic and
    Ridgid RP are the two mainstream options. A JM with strong opinions on press tools
    has used both extensively. No opinion = either new to press or not engaged with
    their tools.
- `[Trade]` You're laying out a building and you don't have a total station. What's your
  workflow? Laser level, chalk, tape, story pole — walk me through it.
  - *Answer context:* standard pre-total-station sprinkler layout = reference off
    architectural column grid, use rotary laser for elevation, chalk line for pipe runs,
    tape + story pole for head locations. Excel Fire's layout work needs to be dialed —
    this is daily work.
  - *Listening for:* they don't freeze at the "no total station" constraint. Real JMs
    worked this way for decades; only the last 10–15 years has total-station use been
    widespread in sprinkler work. Any JM worth hiring can lay out a building with a
    tape, a level, and a chalk box. Bonus if they mention checking against column grid
    AND checking their own work.

### Union & trade-political fluency (Local 669 specific)

- `[Trade]` You're currently [their local] — any history with 669, or with any other
  sprinkler locals? *(open-ended; factual, not evaluative)*
  - *Answer context:* UA Local 669 is the Road Sprinkler Fitters local — nationwide
    jurisdiction for sprinkler fitter work, single local for the whole country. Other
    locals that do sprinkler work include some pipefitter locals that have sprinkler
    jurisdiction (rare) or regional sprinkler-specific locals in certain markets. Most
    signatory sprinkler work in the US is 669.
  - *Listening for:* factual answer about their book history. A 669 member moving
    between contractors within 669 is normal. A member from another trade coming over
    to sprinkler = probe why. Someone with multiple local histories may be either well-
    traveled or someone who gets kicked off books — follow up.
- `[Trade]` What's your take on the current apprenticeship program — strengths and gaps?
  - *Listening for:* engagement with the trade beyond their paycheck. Good answer: names
    a specific strength (rigor, national mobility, pay scale) and a specific gap (newer
    tech not in curriculum, hydraulic-calc training light, ITM side underemphasized).
    Means they've mentored apprentices or thought about the next generation. "I don't
    really follow it" = checked out of the trade-political side.
- `[Trade]` When a contractor gets a JATC inspection, what does that look like from the
  fitter's chair?
  - *Answer context:* JATC (Joint Apprenticeship and Training Committee) inspections
    typically verify apprentice-to-journeyman ratios on jobs, that apprentices are
    getting appropriate scope exposure (not just stuck on one task all year), and that
    the contractor is following the apprenticeship standards. From the fitter's chair:
    usually shows up as a ratio check, a question or two to the apprentice about their
    current scope, and minimal disruption. Doesn't usually impact the JM's day directly
    unless there's a violation.
  - *Listening for:* awareness that this is a thing, and understanding that apprentice
    development is their responsibility as a JM — not just something HR tracks. "I've
    never seen one" = either a new JM, or a contractor that doesn't get inspected =
    possibly a yellow flag on their past employer.
- `[Trade]` Have you worked both signatory and non-signatory shops? If yes, what was the
  biggest practical difference day-to-day?
  - *Answer context:* signatory = union contractor (669 shop), pays scale, union
    benefits, union grievance procedures. Non-signatory = merit shop / non-union;
    typically lower pay but sometimes faster decision-making, less paperwork. Excel Fire
    is signatory 669.
  - *Listening for:* they can articulate real differences without turning it into
    political grandstanding. Good answer: "scale and benefits obviously, but also the
    ratio rules meant I was training apprentices more formally on the signatory side,
    vs. just-watching-me on the non-signatory side." Bad: anti-union or anti-merit
    rants either direction — signal that this person will create friction. NLRA note:
    don't treat the answer as a screening criterion for union sympathy/antipathy.

## 7. Trades-recruiting staples — behavioral / STAR `[Recruit]`

General behavioral questions that work well across blue-collar / skilled-trades hiring.
Pick 2–4 of these for round 1 to supplement Kevin's list; they're designed to produce
concrete stories (STAR: Situation, Task, Action, Result) rather than opinions. Good
interviewers ask follow-ups — "what happened next," "who said what," "how did you know
it worked" — until the candidate is clearly drawing from memory, not inventing.

### Reliability & accountability

- `[Recruit]` Tell me about the best jobsite you've been on in the last five years. What
  made it good — the crew, the foreman, the scope, the customer?
  - *Listening for:* what they value. A candidate who answers "good foreman, crew that
    cared about craft" = values people/craft. "Easy money, no one watching" = values
    slack. Both are honest, and you use the answer to predict how they'll behave at
    Excel Fire (small shop, everyone watches, craft matters).
- `[Recruit]` Tell me about the worst jobsite you've been on in the last five years. Same
  question — what made it bad, and what did you learn from it?
  - *Listening for:* ability to extract lessons from pain. Good answer: specific,
    concrete, ends with "what I now do differently." Bad: just a rant with no takeaway,
    or blames everyone but themselves.
- `[Recruit]` Describe a time you disagreed with a decision a foreman or GC made. What was
  the decision, how did you raise your concern, and what happened?
  - *Listening for:* constructive dissent capability. Good: raised it privately, brought
    evidence (code section, photo, prior experience), accepted the final decision if it
    went against them while documenting their concern. Bad: public confrontation, or
    silent compliance with no attempt to raise it.
- `[Recruit]` Walk me through a time you had to own a mistake that cost the company money.
  How did you handle the conversation with your boss, and what did you change?
  - *Listening for:* ownership + repair. Good: told boss early (before they found out
    the other way), framed as "here's what happened, here's what it cost, here's what
    I'll do differently, here's how I'll make it right." Bad: "nothing I can think of"
    = either green or hiding, or the story is someone else's fault.
- `[Recruit]` When was the last time you were late to a job? What caused it, and how did
  you communicate it?
  - *Listening for:* concrete example (everyone is late sometimes) + proactive
    communication (called the foreman / job, didn't just show up sheepish). Red flag:
    "I'm never late" = likely lying, and the follow-up you can't ask ("how often?")
    is the real question.

### Craftsmanship & pride

- `[Recruit]` What's a piece of work you've done in the last year you're actually proud of?
  Describe it like you're walking a new apprentice through the finished job.
  - *Listening for:* specificity + pride. Good answer has details: sizes, finishes, the
    problem they solved, the piece they'd want photographed. Bad: generic ("just finished
    a nice install") = no emotional relationship to their craft.
- `[Recruit]` What's something on a sprinkler install that separates "meets code" from
  "looks good too"? What do you personally hold yourself to?
  - *Listening for:* trade aesthetics. Good answers: hanger lines straight and equal
    height, couplings clocked consistently, pipe parallel to the architecture (not
    slightly off-axis), escutcheons seated properly, head elevations matching in a
    grid. This is the "I want to look good in the overhead photo" signal. A candidate
    who says "if it passes, it passes" is functional but not a craft hire.
- `[Recruit]` Tell me about a time you reworked something that was technically passing
  because it wasn't up to your standard. Who paid for the rework — you, the boss, or the
  customer?
  - *Listening for:* pride-driven rework vs. no-rework-ever. Good answer: did it on their
    own time or talked the boss into it because they'd see it every time they walked
    past. Bad: "I just move on if it passes" = functional but not Excel's culture.

### Teamwork & communication

- `[Recruit]` Describe the best foreman you've ever worked for and the worst. Don't name
  names — just what made them what they were.
  - *Listening for:* leadership values. Good answer: specific behaviors ("clear scope
    every morning, defended the crew to the GC, admitted when he was wrong" vs. "vague,
    threw the crew under the bus, blamed downward"). Reveals what they'll emulate and
    what they'll avoid if they ever foreman themselves.
- `[Recruit]` Tell me about training an apprentice. What do you teach first, and what do
  you hold back until they're ready?
  - *Listening for:* teaching instinct + patience. Good answer: safety first, then tool
    handling, then basic pipe work, then reading drawings, then letting them make
    controlled mistakes. Hold back: anything with fall exposure or live systems. Bad:
    "I just tell them to watch me" = not a trainer = apprentices will hate them and
    leave.
- `[Recruit]` When a customer or GC is frustrated with you or the crew, what's your
  instinct — defuse it yourself, escalate to the foreman, or call the office?
  - *Listening for:* calibration. Good answer: try to defuse in the moment if it's
    scope-adjacent or relational, escalate to foreman if it's changing the work, call
    the office if it's contract-adjacent. Bad: one-size-fits-all (always escalate or
    always handle it alone) = missing social intelligence.
- `[Recruit]` How do you handle a crew member who's not pulling their weight? Walk me
  through the last time that happened.
  - *Listening for:* graduated escalation. Good: talked to them directly first, gave them
    a chance to explain (maybe something's going on at home), gave pointed feedback on
    the specific behavior, looped in the foreman if it didn't improve. Bad: went
    straight to the foreman without talking to them, or tolerated it indefinitely and
    resented them.

### Decision-making under pressure

- `[Recruit]` Tell me about a time a job went sideways and you had to make a call in the
  moment without the boss. What was the call, and how did it land?
  - *Listening for:* autonomy + judgment. Good answer: made a reasonable call, documented
    it, told the boss as soon as possible afterward, accepted it if the boss would've
    decided differently. Bad: froze and waited for the boss to magically appear, or made
    a call and hid it.
- `[Recruit]` A customer tries to get you to do something that isn't in the scope —
  sometimes at the end of the day — for a hundred bucks cash. What do you do?
  - *Listening for:* the integrity question in disguise. Right answer: polite no, refer to
    the office, don't take cash for work because it creates liability (who covers the
    warranty? who pays if the candidate damages something?) and legal exposure (off-the-
    books income, tax). Wrong answer: "I'd do it" = will steal from Excel in small ways
    for the rest of their employment. Gray answer: "I'd take the cash and add it to
    the ticket" = working toward the right instinct but not there yet.
- `[Recruit]` Emergency callout at 2 AM — burst head, water pouring through a ceiling.
  Walk me through your first 30 minutes on site.
  - *Listening for:* crisis sequence. Good answer: call the customer en route with
    estimated arrival, first-on-scene shuts the isolation valve, calls monitoring to
    silence/hold the alarm panel, locates a mop and buckets if nothing else to stem
    immediate damage, assesses scope. Pairs with the flood story. Bad: launches into
    the repair before stopping the water.

### Motivation & fit

- `[Recruit]` What made you pick fire sprinklers over general plumbing, pipefitting, or
  mechanical? How did you land in this trade?
  - *Listening for:* agency in their career vs. drift. Good: a deliberate choice with a
    reason (family member in the trade, liked the life-safety angle, liked the pay and
    national mobility). Drift: "I just fell into it" = okay but probes lower commitment.
- `[Recruit]` If the right offer came in from a GC shop tomorrow, what would it take for
  you to leave the sprinkler side?
  - *Listening for:* lifer vs. tourist. Strong answer: "I like the sprinkler side, the
    life-safety mission matters to me, it'd take a huge jump to pull me." Tourist
    answer: "Right pay, I'm gone." You want someone who sees themselves in the trade
    for the next decade.
- `[Recruit]` What's a trade skill you want to be better at a year from now? What's
  stopping you?
  - *Listening for:* growth orientation. Good answer names a specific skill (layout in
    Revit, hydraulics, pre-action systems, tig welding, running a crew) and a concrete
    reason it hasn't happened yet (shop doesn't do it, no mentor, haven't made time).
    Bad: "I'm good where I am" = complacent.
- `[Recruit]` Pay, benefits, location, crew quality, work type — rank them by what matters
  most to you right now.
  - *Listening for:* what actually motivates them — so you know how to pitch the offer
    and where Excel Fire is strong/weak vs. their preferences. Also listening for
    consistency: does the ranking match their other answers in the interview? Pay-first
    is fine and honest; location-first reads as a relocation match; crew-first reads as
    someone who'll mesh in a small shop like Excel.

### Closing questions (give the candidate a chance to sell themselves)

- `[Recruit]` What's something I should have asked you that I didn't?
  - *Listening for:* self-awareness about their gaps + chance to volunteer something
    positive. Good answer brings up a concern proactively ("you didn't ask about the
    gap in my resume in 2023, happy to explain") or a strength they didn't get to
    showcase. Bad: "nothing comes to mind" = low self-awareness or low engagement.
- `[Recruit]` What questions do you have for me — about the role, the company, me?
  - *Listening for:* real questions signal engagement and due diligence. Red flag: no
    questions at all. Yellow flag: only money/benefits questions — legitimate but
    suggests they haven't thought about fit. Good: mix of practical (pay, benefits,
    schedule) and substantive (growth path, typical week, crew dynamics, why I'm hiring
    now).
- `[Recruit]` If we made you an offer today and you accepted, what's the first thing
  you'd want to know before your first Monday?
  - *Listening for:* practical first-day thinking. Good answer: scope of their first
    week's work, who to report to, what tools Excel provides vs. what they bring, dress
    code / PPE expectations, paperwork/onboarding schedule, starting wage on the first
    check. Bad: no answer = not thinking about actually showing up, or a laundry list
    of demands = negotiation-first mindset.

## Proposed 4-stage interview process

Kevin explicitly wants round 1 phone-solo (Justin only) and round 2 Zoom (Justin + Kevin +
Keith). Adding a stage 3 working interview is the single most-effective change for
blue-collar hiring — it cuts bad hires dramatically because the candidate performs actual
work in front of the crew and the crew gets veto input. It's also what Kevin is really
after when he complains Keith hired "shitheads" — a phone/Zoom screen can't catch what a
day in the shop catches.

### Stage 1 — 30-minute phone screen (Justin solo)

*Goal: is this a real JM with a plausible story, and do they actually want to move to
Marquette?*

**Time budget (30 min total):**
- 0–3 min: warm open. "Thanks for the application. I saw X on your resume." Tell them
  the plan for the call.
- 3–8 min: Kevin's work-history questions — years as JM, years at current employer,
  project types as foreman, reference self-assessment.
- 8–18 min: 5–6 behavioral questions pulled from §1 Work ethic, §2 Honesty, §3 UP-growth.
  Tailor picks to the candidate's HireScore profile. At least one `[Recruit]` STAR
  question (craftsmanship or reliability).
- 18–23 min: Kevin's "why UP" question + §5 Relocation behavioral (winter experience,
  long-drive service work, timeline to start).
- 23–28 min: Company Cam / tech-adoption block from §4. At least two questions.
- 28–30 min: candidate Q&A + close. "Next step if we move forward is a Zoom with me,
  Kevin Masich (current owner), and Keith LeFebvre (GM). You'd do maybe 30 min of tech
  screen + Q&A together. After that, if we're both still interested, we'd fly you up for
  a paid day in the shop."

*Pass/fail bar:* if 3+ of the 5 signals come back "yes, looks real," move to stage 2. If
2 or fewer, send a polite "thanks but moving on" — no stage 2. Document the decision in
the debrief template.

### Stage 2 — 45-minute Zoom (Justin + Kevin + Keith + candidate)

*Goal: (a) does the candidate look and sound like a JM Kevin would hire, (b) do they
pass a real trade screen, (c) does Keith think they'll mesh with the existing crew.*

**Time budget (45 min total):**
- 0–5 min: intros all around. Kevin and Keith say a few words about the shop.
- 5–25 min: **Trade-knowledge screen** — 5–7 questions from §6 `[Trade]`, weighted
  toward systems they'll actually work on at Excel Fire (western UP is heavy on
  occupied commercial + limited industrial). Kevin leads this block; Justin and Keith
  interject with follow-ups.
- 25–35 min: Keith's block — crew dynamics, typical week at Excel, who they'd be working
  alongside. Fitter-to-fitter stuff. 2–3 questions from §7 `[Recruit]` Teamwork block.
- 35–43 min: candidate Q&A. This is where they sell themselves and ask the stuff that
  matters to them (pay, benefits, housing in Marquette, school districts, whatever).
- 43–45 min: close. "Next step if we all feel good is a paid working day in the shop.
  We'll fly you up Tuesday, you spend the day with Keith and the crew, we put you on real
  work, we all get a feel for how it goes. Paid JM scale + lodging + meals. No obligation
  on either side."

*Pass/fail bar:* all three interviewers (Justin, Kevin, Keith) have to be a "yes" to
move to stage 3. If any one of them is a firm no, that's the decision. Tie goes to Keith
because he's the one working alongside the candidate post-hire.

### Stage 3 — 1-day paid working interview in Marquette

*Goal: see the candidate actually fit, thread, test, and talk to customers — and let the
crew tell us if they want to work with this person.*

**Logistics:**
- Fly them up Monday night, work Tuesday 7:00–15:30 with the crew, fly home Tuesday
  evening. Excel pays for flight, hotel (Superior Stay Hotel in Marquette — per Quo
  contacts), rental car, meals, and 8 hours at Local 669 JM scale *regardless of
  outcome*. That's ~$1,200–1,500 all-in per candidate. It's a rounding error against the
  cost of a bad JM hire.
- Frame it to the candidate honestly: "This is not a test in the sense of 'do perfect
  work and you're in.' It's a mutual try-out. We want you to see what working at Excel
  looks like. We want to see how you work. No one's trying to trick anyone."
- Have them work on a representative mix: 2 hours on an ITM site, 4 hours on an active
  install or service call, 2 hours back at the shop on fab or paperwork. Rotate them
  through at least 2 crew members during the day.
- Keith or Kevin takes them to lunch 1-on-1. That's where the relocation reality
  conversation happens organically — where would they live, what's their spouse's read,
  what do they need to make the move work.

**Evaluation:**
- Each crew member who worked with them fills out a 5-question debrief form at end of
  day: (1) would you work with this person again tomorrow? (2) did they know what they
  were doing? (3) did they ask good questions? (4) did they talk down to anyone?
  (5) anything weird?
- Keith collates and reports to Justin + Kevin within 24 hours.

*Pass/fail bar:* unanimous "yes" from the crew members who worked with them. One clear
"no" from a fitter who spent meaningful time with them kills the hire — that's the whole
point of the stage. Ambiguous signal = second working day before deciding.

### Stage 4 — reference check + offer

*Goal: verify what they said in stage 1 and surface any late surprises before the offer.*

- Call 2–3 professional references. Kevin said on the 04-17 call he would tell candidates
  he *wasn't* going to call their current employer — keep that promise. Call past
  employers, union BAs from their local, or a past foreman they volunteered as a
  reference.
- Use the reference call script below — it's short, open-ended, and legally safe.
- Pull a Local 669 work history verification from the international if possible
  (confirm JM status, book status, any safety flags).
- Run a background check *after* contingent offer is extended (ban-the-box best practice;
  Michigan doesn't mandate it but it's cleaner).
- Drug test per Excel's standard policy.
- Offer letter covers base, benefits, relocation allowance (if any), start date, 90-day
  review cadence.

**Reference call script (3 min, candidate-volunteered references only):**

1. *"Hi, this is Justin at Excel Fire Protection in Marquette. [Candidate] gave me your
   name as a reference. Got 2–3 minutes?"*
2. *"What was your working relationship — foreman, coworker, GC?"*
3. *"Would you work with [candidate] again if you had an opening tomorrow?"* — the
   single most-predictive reference-check question in the literature. Listen to the
   pause more than the answer.
4. *"Anything I should know that I probably won't find on a resume?"*
5. *"Any reason they shouldn't be doing sprinkler work in a JM role?"*
6. Close.

Never ask a reference for medical info, arrest/conviction info, demographic info, or
anything else you couldn't ask the candidate directly. Same EEOC/ADA rules apply to
references.

### Stage arc summary

| Stage | Length | Who | Primary goal | Cost | Kill rate target |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Phone screen | 30 min | Justin | Plausibility + UP fit | ~$25 (Justin time) | 40–60% of applicants |
| 2. Zoom | 45 min | J + Kevin + Keith | Trade depth + crew vibe | ~$125 (3 salaries × 1 hr) | 40–60% of stage-1 passes |
| 3. Working day | 1 day | J + Kevin + Keith + crew + candidate | Real fit + crew veto | ~$1,200–1,500 all-in | 10–30% of stage-2 passes |
| 4. References + offer | ~1 week | Justin | Verify + close | ~$500 (BG check, drug test, admin) | 0–10% (late kill rare) |

*Expected funnel:* 10 applicants → ~5 stage-1 passes → ~2–3 stage-2 passes → 1 working
day → 1 offer. The working-day stage is the bottleneck by design — it's expensive but
catches the hires that phone/Zoom miss, and cycle 7239 is small enough (2 candidates
today) that the cost is trivial.

## Red-flag list — questions to NEVER ask, with safe alternatives

If Kevin (or anyone else) suggests any of these, redirect to the behavioral alternative.
The frame: we're hiring for *ability to do the job in Marquette*, not for demographic fit.

### ⚠️ Kevin-specific landmines (flagged from the 04-17 and 04-18 calls)

Kevin is an older-generation tradesman running a family-style shop — his instincts are
honest and relationship-driven, but three of his specific questions cross EEOC/ADA/ELCRA
lines. Each has an EEOC-safe alternative above that gets the same signal.

1. **"Are you married?"** *(Kevin, call AC e1ca174b, 04-17, 1:53)*
   - **Why it's a problem:** Michigan ELCRA explicitly protects marital status. Title VII
     + PDA also create risk on the family-status inference.
   - **What Kevin is actually trying to learn:** stability, long-term intent,
     willingness to relocate family.
   - **EEOC-safe replacement** *(already in §3 / §5 above):* "Why would you want to move
     yourself and your family to the UP?" — open-ended, candidate self-discloses whatever
     they want. You never ask whether a family exists.

2. **"Do you have any health issues?"** *(Kevin, call AC 8f966c48, 04-18, 0:11)*
   - **Why it's a problem:** flat ADA pre-offer violation. No exceptions.
   - **What Kevin is actually trying to learn:** can this person physically do the
     work, and are there hidden reliability concerns.
   - **EEOC-safe replacement** *(already in §2 above):* describe the essential functions
     of the job (ladders, lifting, attics, 10-hr days) and ask "can you perform these
     duties with or without reasonable accommodation?" Post-offer, a job-specific physical
     does the rest.

3. **"Have you ever had a drinking or drug problem?"** *(Kevin, call AC 8f966c48, 04-18,
   0:15)*
   - **Why it's a problem:** past addiction is a protected disability under ADA. Current
     illegal use is *not* protected, but you still can't ask — you drug-test.
   - **What Kevin is actually trying to learn:** is this person a risk to show up
     impaired or go off the rails.
   - **EEOC-safe replacement** *(already in §2 above):* state Excel Fire's drug-free
     workplace policy and drug-testing program (pre-employment, post-incident,
     reasonable-suspicion) and ask if they have any concerns with that. The test does
     the screening; the question doesn't have to.

### Age / generation

- **Don't ask:** "How old are you?" / "When did you graduate high school?" / "How many
  years until you retire?" / "Are you comfortable working with younger people?"
- **Why:** ADEA (federal) + Michigan ELCRA both protect 40+ from age discrimination.
  Graduation-year math gives away age.
- **Safe alternative:** *"How long do you see yourself doing sprinkler fitter work
  specifically? What's the long-term arc for you?"* — gets at tenure intent without age.

### Marital / family status

- See Kevin-specific landmine #1 above. Also:
- **Don't ask:** "Do you have kids?" / "Are you planning to have kids?" / "Can you work
  late with your family situation?" / "Who watches your kids if you're on call?"
- **Safe alternative:** *"This job includes on-call rotation roughly every 6 weeks and
  occasional overnight travel to the western UP. Is that compatible with your situation?"*
  — states the requirement, lets them self-assess without disclosing family info.

### National origin / citizenship

- **Don't ask:** "Where are you from?" / "Where are your parents from?" / "What's your
  first language?" / "You have an accent — where's that from?"
- **Why:** Title VII national origin protection. Also IRCA.
- **Safe alternative (legal screening):** *"Are you authorized to work in the United
  States? Would you now or in the future require visa sponsorship?"* — those two questions
  are the *only* citizenship-adjacent questions that are safe, and you ask them of every
  candidate.

### Religion

- **Don't ask:** "Do you go to church?" / "What religion are you?" / "Can you work
  Sundays?" (as a standalone — only ask if Sunday work is genuinely required, and then
  frame it as availability.)
- **Why:** Title VII, ELCRA. Even "innocent" religion questions create discrimination
  risk.
- **Safe alternative:** *"The job sometimes requires weekend service calls — typically 1
  or 2 Saturdays a month. Any reason that would be a problem?"* — availability, not
  religion.

### Disability / health

- See Kevin-specific landmine #2 above for the health-issues variant.

### Pregnancy

- **Don't ask:** *anything.*
- **Why:** PDA.
- **Safe alternative:** don't go there. Job requirements + availability questions cover it.

### Military / veteran status

- **Don't ask:** "What kind of discharge did you receive?" / "Did you see combat?" /
  "Are you in the Reserves?" (reserves question can screen out service members doing
  drill weekends, which is USERRA-protected.)
- **Why:** USERRA, state veteran-status protections.
- **Safe alternative:** *"We value military experience — do you have any skills or
  training from military service that might apply to this role?"* — opens the door, lets
  them volunteer what they want. And if Reserves service is a real concern: *"This
  position has rotating on-call weekends. Are you generally available on weekends?"* —
  availability framing.

### Criminal history

- **Don't ask:** "Have you ever been arrested?"
- **Why:** Arrests without convictions can't be used; asking invites adverse-impact
  claims. Also, several states (not currently MI, but movement in this space) ban-the-box
  pre-offer.
- **Safe alternative:** *"Have you ever been convicted of a felony? If yes, please
  explain."* — conviction-specific, with an opportunity to explain, and only consider it
  if it's job-related.

### Union history (as a disqualifier)

- **Don't ask:** "Why did you leave the union last time?" in a way that telegraphs you're
  screening out union organizers.
- **Why:** NLRA. Especially relevant at Excel Fire, which is a *signatory* 669 shop —
  "leaving a non-signatory contractor for a signatory one" is a legitimate conversation
  but don't frame anything as anti-union.
- **Safe alternative:** *"Walk me through your union history — you're Local 669 currently,
  yes? Any history with other locals?"* — factual, not evaluative.

### "UP culture fit" framed as demographics

- **Don't ask:** "Are you a Yooper?" / "How do you feel about being around mostly white
  rural folks?" / "Are you religious? The UP can be churchy."
- **Why:** proxies for race / religion / national origin. Even if asked innocently, these
  create evidence of discriminatory intent.
- **Safe alternative:** the "willingness to relocate" behavioral questions above cover
  this safely. The answer you're actually looking for — do they get what Marquette is and
  still want the job — comes out of "what pulled you to the UP?" and "have you spent time
  here?" type questions, not identity questions.
